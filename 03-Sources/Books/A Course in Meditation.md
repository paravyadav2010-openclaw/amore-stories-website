### Summary
#### Introduction Summary

The introduction frames this book as a practical "21-day workout" designed specifically for the hyperactive modern mind, which often struggles to relax into silence. It explains that traditional meditation can be difficult for contemporary people because of the constant noise and distractions of the 21st century. To address this, Osho offers techniques that help separate the self from the mind, leading to a transformation of consciousness. The goal of the course is not just to practice techniques but to reclaim a lost natural quality—a state of alertness and responsiveness to life that we all possess but have forgotten.

##### Key Points
- **Designed for the Modern Mind**: Acknowledges that the busy, hyperactive nature of modern life makes traditional silence difficult, requiring a new approach.
- **Reclaiming Nature**: Meditation is presented as a return to our natural state, not an artificial addition. It is about "reclaiming the meditative nature" we were born with but lost through social conditioning.
- **Structure of the Course**: The book is organized into a 21-day program. Each day includes an insight on a specific topic (like love, anger, or relaxation) followed by a practical awareness exercise.
- **Transformation**: The ultimate aim is a shift in consciousness where you learn to "separate yourself from your mind" and live with greater alertness and joy.

##### Actionable Tips
- **Commit to the Process**: View the next 21 days as a structured "workout" for your consciousness, just as you would for your body.
- **Use the Tools**: Be ready to read the daily insight, practice the specific technique, and reflect on the quote of the day.
- **Don't Just Read**: The introduction emphasises that this is a *course*—participation and practice are required to experience the shift, not just intellectual understanding.

#### Day 1 Summary: What Is Meditation?

Day 1 introduces meditation not as a technique to learn, but as a natural quality we are born with—a state of pure awareness and presence that gets covered up through social conditioning and mental habits. Osho explains that children are born in a meditative state, simply present and aware, but as we teach them the ways of the world, this quality becomes polluted and lost. The practice of meditation is about reclaiming this original state of consciousness. When we experience even brief moments ("two drops") of genuine awareness, we recognise it as something we've been seeking, and this taste motivates us to make it a constant state of being.

##### Key Points
- **Natural State**: Meditation is a quality of awareness and presence that we are born with, not something external to acquire.
- **Childhood Conditioning**: Children naturally exist in a meditative state until social conditioning and mental patterns overlay this original awareness.
- **Love and Nourishment**: The connection between love and nourishment is fundamental; when a baby is born, the mother's breast provides both food and love. A lack of love often manifests as overeating in adults.
- **Reconnection**: The work is to remember and reconnect with this innate quality rather than learning something entirely new.
- **"Two Drops"**: Even small experiences of genuine presence reveal what we're truly seeking and motivate continued practice.

##### Main Framework
- **Awareness is Meditation**: The essential quality is presence and watchfulness, not the specific activity you're doing.
- **Conscious Action**: The same daily activities become meditation when done with full awareness rather than automatically.
- **Watch Without Judgment**: Simply observe what is happening in your body, mind, and surroundings without trying to change it.
- **Return Again and Again**: When you drift into thought, the practice is noticing and gently coming back to presence.

##### Examples
- **Walking**: Be fully aware of each step, the sensations in your body, and the sights and sounds around you, staying present in the moment.
- **Picking Up Objects**: Feel the texture, weight, temperature, and shape consciously—experience the full sensory reality rather than handling things mechanically.
- **Eating**: Chew with awareness, taste fully, and notice hunger and satisfaction signals.

##### Exercise for Day 1
- Choose ordinary daily activities and practice doing them with complete awareness rather than mechanically.
- When walking, feel your feet touching the ground and notice your surroundings.
- When handling objects, bring full attention to the sensory experience (weight, texture, temperature).
- Practice being the watcher: observe your actions, thoughts, and sensations without judgment or interference.


#### Day 2 Summary: Meditations on Love and Relating

Day 2 explores the crucial distinction between "relationship" and "relating." Osho explains that a relationship is something complete, finished, and closed—it is based on taking things for granted and falling into automatic patterns of behaviour. In contrast, relating is alive, fresh, and dynamic; it involves meeting the person as if for the first time every single moment. When we drop the habit of seeing someone as a fixed entity (based on past expectations), we open ourselves to a genuine encounter. This shift from relationship to relating is where true intimacy, love, and meditation intersect. The meditation exercises for Day 2 are designed to bring awareness and presence into our connections with others, breaking through the mechanical, habitual ways we typically interact.

##### Key Points
- **Relationship vs Relating**: A relationship is static and based on past conditioning; relating is alive, present, and renews itself moment to moment.
- **Taking for Granted**: In relationships, we often take our partner or friend for granted, assuming we know them completely, which closes off genuine connection.
- **Meeting Afresh**: True relating means encountering the person anew in each moment, dropping assumptions and past narratives about who they are.
- **Meditation as Foundation**: Love cannot exist without meditation; love grounded in awareness becomes authentic and transforms both people involved.
- **Breaking Automaticity**: Our habitual patterns and conditioned responses prevent us from truly seeing and being with another person.

##### Main Framework
- **Awareness in Connection**: Apply the meditative quality of presence to your relationships, not just to solitary practice.
- **Release Expectations**: Let go of who you think the person is and meet them fresh, as if encountering them for the first time.
- **Two-Way Witnessing**: Both partners practise witnessing themselves and each other without judgment or attachment to outcomes.
- **Energy Exchange**: Through sustained eye contact and hand-holding, energy flows and merges between two conscious beings.

##### Examples
- Seeing your partner: Instead of unconsciously assuming you know them, pause and truly observe them as if you've never met before.
- Holding hands: Feel the subtle warmth, texture, and energy flowing through the palms rather than holding hands as a mechanical gesture.
- Eye contact in daily life: When speaking to someone, meet their eyes fully, noticing what is actually present in this moment with them.

##### Exercises for Day 2

###### Meditation One (Solo)
- Sit quietly and bring to mind someone close to you (partner, friend, or family member).
- Observe your automatic thoughts and judgements about them without acting on them.
- Consciously release your expectations and mental image of who they "should be."
- Feel what it is like to be open to them as a fresh, unknown person in each moment.

###### Meditation Two (With a Partner or Friend)
This meditation has three stages and lasts approximately 30 minutes total.

**Stage One: Eye Gazing (10 minutes)**
- Sit facing each other, close enough to see each other's eyes clearly, at a comfortable distance (arm's length apart, preferably).
- Hold each other's hands crosswise (your left hand holding their left hand, your right hand holding their right hand).
- Look directly into each other's eyes for the full 10 minutes, allowing your gaze to soften and relax.
- You may blink naturally, but maintain continuous eye contact.
- If your body starts to move or sway, allow it to happen—do not resist natural movements.
- Do not let go of each other's hands, no matter what arises.

**Stage Two: Eyes Closed, Sitting (10 minutes)**
- Close your eyes whilst still sitting facing each other.
- Continue holding each other's hands in the same crosswise position.
- Allow any swaying or gentle body movements to occur without restraint.
- Feel the energy moving between you both; simply witness what happens without trying to control it.

**Stage Three: Standing and Swaying Together (10 minutes)**
- Stand up slowly, still holding hands in the same crosswise grip.
- Allow your bodies to sway and move together in whatever way feels natural.
- This movement may evolve into a gentle dance as energy continues to flow.
- Keep your eyes open during this final stage, remaining connected through both hands and gaze.
- Feel the merging and mixing of your energies with the other person.

##### Actionable Tips
- **Practise with Awareness**: Choose someone you are close to and commit to seeing them freshly each day, suspending all assumptions.
- **Slow Down Interactions**: In conversations, pause before speaking and truly look at the person—notice what changes in you when you do this.
- **Release the Story**: When you catch yourself thinking "they always do this" or "they are this type of person," gently remind yourself that you are meeting them anew.
- **Do the Partner Meditation Regularly**: Practising the eye-gazing and hand-holding exercise weekly (or even daily) deepens the capacity to relate authentically.
- **Notice Energy Shifts**: After completing the meditation with a partner, reflect on how your sense of connection changed and what barriers dissolved.




#### Day 3 Summary: Anger and the Watcher

Day 3 explores anger as a powerful energy that usually controls us because we either explode (express it) or push it down (repress it). Osho explains that both expression and repression keep anger alive and strengthen its grip over us. Instead, he points to a “third way”: becoming a silent watcher. From this perspective, anger is something that arises in you, moves through you, and disappears again, while you remain separate, like someone watching from a distance. The key is to stop feeding anger with your energy and identification. When you don’t cooperate with it, anger cannot do anything, and what remains afterwards is a deep stillness.

##### Key Points
- Anger itself is a form of raw energy; it is not “bad” in essence, but unconscious anger is destructive.
- Most people only know two options with anger: to express it outwardly or to repress it inwardly.
- Expressing anger on others creates a chain reaction and turns anger into a habit.
- Repressing anger pushes it into the unconscious, where it becomes stored poison and can explode later in more dangerous ways.
- The third way is neither to express nor to repress, but to watch—remaining aware of anger without acting on it.
- When you stop pouring energy into anger through thoughts, stories, and identification, it loses its power and dissolves.
- After anger passes under the light of awareness, a surprising sense of peace and clarity can appear.

##### Four Laws / Main Framework
- **Neither Express nor Repress**  
  The usual two methods both fail: expression hurts you and others, repression hurts you from within. The meditative way avoids both extremes.
  
- **Be the Watcher, Not the Anger**  
  When anger arises, imagine sitting a little distance away from it. You are the one who sees it, not the one who is it. This shift from “I am angry” to “Anger is happening” is the heart of the practice.
  
- **Energy Needs Cooperation**  
  Anger becomes strong only when you feed it with inner dialogue, fantasies of revenge, and dramatic stories. By simply observing, you withdraw this cooperation, and the energy burns out on its own.
  
- **From Turbulence to Stillness**  
  If you stay present and don’t act from the anger, it will peak and then fall. In the silence that follows, you get a direct taste of meditation—freshness, lightness, and a sense of inner space.

##### Examples
- You feel triggered by someone’s comment: instead of shouting back (expression) or forcing yourself to “be nice” while boiling inside (repression), you say nothing for a moment, feel the heat and tightness in your body, and just observe it.
- You notice angry thoughts repeating: “They always do this… I’ll show them…” You recognise these as ways of feeding the anger and gently let the stories go while staying with the raw sensation of anger itself.
- During an outburst that is already happening, you suddenly realise, “Oh, this is anger,” and for a few seconds you step back internally and watch. Even a brief moment of witnessing starts cooling the fire.

##### Exercise for Day 3

- Set aside about 20 minutes when you will not be disturbed. Go into a room, close and lock the door so you feel completely safe and alone.
- Bring to mind situations and people that trigger your anger. Allow the anger to rise; come to a real “boiling point” inside.
- Let the feeling of anger become as strong as possible in your body and mind—without directing it at anyone and without acting it out on the outside.
- Now, at the peak, do not express and do not repress: simply sit (or stand) and *watch* the anger as if from far away.
- Notice where it sits in the body, how it moves, how thoughts try to fan it. Keep returning to the position of an observer.
- Remain with this watching until the anger naturally begins to subside. Stay present for the quietness that follows.

##### Actionable Tips
- When anger starts, silently name it: “Anger is here,” instead of “I am angry.” This helps create distance.
- Delay action: give yourself even 20–60 seconds just to watch before speaking or doing anything.
- Pay attention to body sensations (heat, tightness, heartbeat) more than to angry thoughts; the body is easier to observe without getting lost.
- After each episode of anger, briefly reflect: “Was there even one moment when I became a watcher?”—this strengthens the witnessing habit.
- Practise the 20-minute locked-room exercise regularly (for example, a few times a week) to learn how to stay with strong anger without expressing or repressing it.
